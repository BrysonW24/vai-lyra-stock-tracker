
Comprehensive Chart Dashboard for Traders
Traders benefit from a dashboard that spans global macroeconomics, market structure, commodities, and portfolio metrics. Central banks and analysts already publish rich chart packs – for example, the RBA’s Chart Pack includes dozens of graphs on GDP, consumption, inflation, employment, credit, commodities, interest rates, and equity indices. In practice, key data series include leading indicators (GDP growth, manufacturing PMI, building permits), coincident data (retail sales, industrial production), and lagging data (unemployment rate, corporate profits). As Investopedia notes, the most important economic indicators for investors are things like stock indexes, unemployment claims, housing starts, GDP, and consumer confidence. Charting these together helps reveal the current economic cycle and warn of inflection points.

Global & Domestic Macroeconomic Indicators
GDP Growth (Real GDP) – total output growth (YoY or quarter-on-quarter) is the primary barometer of economic health.
Employment & Labor – unemployment rate and weekly jobless claims show labor-market conditions and lag the business cycle.
Inflation (CPI/PCE) – consumer price indexes (headline and core inflation) track purchasing power and guide monetary policy.
Consumer & Business Sentiment – surveys such as consumer confidence and manufacturing PMIs can lead turns in spending and investment.
Housing & Construction – housing starts, building permits and home sales affect consumer wealth and materials demand.
Money & Credit – broad money supply (M2/M3) and credit growth (bank lending, household debt) indicate liquidity and leverage trends.
These charts correspond to sections in official “chart packs” (e.g. the RBA Pack’s sections on GDP, inflation, households and credit). Monitoring the combination of these indicators (e.g. when high-frequency sentiment, PMIs and yield curves all shift) gives advance notice of trend changes.

Financial Market & Liquidity Indicators
Yield Curves – e.g. 10-year vs 2-year or 10-year vs 3-month Treasury spreads. Inversion of the yield curve has preceded every U.S. recession in recent decades, making it a critical chart.
Interest Rates – key policy rates (Fed Funds rate, RBA cash rate, etc.) versus inflation or money-market yields, to gauge real rates.
Credit Spreads – the spread of corporate bond yields (investment-grade and high-yield) over Treasuries. Widening spreads often lead equity sell-offs. (As Schwab notes, “Credit spreads sometimes rise before the stock market begins to show signs of cracking”.)
Financial Stress Index – composite stress gauges (like the St. Louis Fed’s Financial Stress Index) combine credit, volatility and liquidity metrics. Spikes in this index foreshadow market turmoil.
Stock Market Breadth and Indices – major indexes (S&P 500, ASX 200, MSCI World, etc.) and breadth measures (advancers vs decliners, new highs) show market internals.
Volatility Measures – VIX (equity volatility) or MOVE (bond volatility) indicate risk sentiment and expected turbulence.
Liquidity & Money Supply – charts of Fed/CB balance sheet size, repo rates or money aggregates (M2) reveal monetary accommodation.
Professional dashboards often include these. For example, traders use FRED data to plot indices of leading indicators, yield curves and stress indices. In the RBA Chart Pack, analogous categories are “Interest Rates” and “Financial System” charts. Together, these charts show whether financial conditions are tightening or easing alongside economic data.

Commodity & Sector Trend Charts
Energy Prices – Brent and WTI oil prices, and natural gas/LNG prices. Energy charts often drive inflation and trade balances.
Industrial Metals – copper price is viewed as an economic “bellwether” (strong demand in construction and manufacturing). Charts of copper (and other base metals) vs time (or vs Chinese consumption) highlight infrastructure demand.
Battery & Tech Metals – prices of lithium, nickel, cobalt, etc. (used in batteries and semiconductors) are increasingly important. Charting these raw-materials prices can signal the health of high-tech and clean-energy sectors.
Precious Metals – gold and silver serve as currency hedges and safe havens. A rising gold chart often signals risk-off sentiment or negative real rates.
Bulk Commodities & Commodity Indexes – iron ore and bulk commodity price indexes (e.g. RBA’s commodity price index or broad indexes like the CRB). For resource-heavy economies, the terms of trade (export prices versus import prices) chart is crucial.
Energy & Infrastructure Demand – charts of power consumption or data-center growth (e.g. BloombergNEF’s projections) highlight surging energy use. These aren’t traditional stock metrics, but they show underlying demand drivers (as Nomi Prins discusses, “data centers and high-heat industries” are driving a new energy surge).
Sector/Industry Indexes – sector ETFs or indices (Energy, Technology, Materials) and their relative performance versus the market help track rotations.
In sum, commodity and sector charts give a view of supply/demand in critical industries. Analysts regularly point to copper charts (our own Portfolio product’s chart pack shows copper vs China demand) and energy consumption charts as leading indicators of broader trends. The RBA pack itself dedicates a section to “Commodity Prices” with charts of oil, LNG, metals and terms of trade.

Portfolio & Stock-Level Metrics
Portfolio Equity Curve & P/L – a time-series chart of your account value or total P/L. This shows drawdowns and growth of capital. Many dashboards compute a rolling Sharpe or drawdown on this curve.
Risk & Performance Stats – charts of portfolio volatility, annualized return, Sharpe ratio or beta (often as a small table or bar chart). For example, the Tradeski dashboard plots Sharpe, beta and vol based on the last year’s returns.
Allocation Breakdown – pie or bar charts of current exposure by sector, country, or theme (e.g. % in tech vs energy). This highlights concentration risk.
Correlation Heatmap – a correlation matrix of portfolio holdings over a recent window (e.g. 90 days) helps visualize diversification. (Tradeski includes a 90-day correlation heatmap as an example.)
Individual Stock Charts – price charts for each ticker on your watchlist, with technical overlays (candlestick or line charts plus RSI, MACD, moving averages, etc.). These help in timing entries/exits.
Benchmark Comparison – overlay portfolio return vs a benchmark index (e.g. S&P 500) to see relative performance and drawdowns.
Watchlist Performance – mini-charts or sparklines showing how watchlist stocks are moving intraday or over time relative to the market.
In practice, personalized dashboards (like the one described on Reddit) combine live candlestick charts with a macro data ribbon and portfolio stats. For instance, such a dashboard might show, side by side, the stock price of a ticker (with RSI/MACD overlays) and your portfolio’s P/L, Sharpe and beta. This integrates the micro (stock chart) with the macro (“ribbon” of CPI, GDP, Fed funds, yield curve) and the portfolio-level view.

Putting It Together
The “Charts” section of the app should be mobile-optimized but rich. A user might swipe through tabs like Economy, Markets, Commodities, and Portfolio. Each tab contains a curated set of charts from the categories above. For example, the Economy tab would include GDP growth, CPI, jobs, and leading indexes; Markets would include yield curves, credit spreads, and volatility; Commodities would show oil, copper, metals indexes; and Portfolio would show P/L and allocations. This mirrors professional chart packs (RBA, BIS, Fed) and advanced dashboards.

By continuously updating these charts, a trader gets a situational awareness of current and emerging conditions: Are yields inverting? Is credit tightening? Are commodity prices spiking? Answering these helps decide whether a stock’s move is driven by fundamental trends or is likely to reverse. In short, combining macro charts (RBA-style) with market charts and personal portfolio charts gives the most educated, contextual view for trading decisions.
